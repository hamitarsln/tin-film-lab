# Feature launch film: rules to try

Drawn from the completed sampled visual/story review of 458 distinct edits. Each rule below cites a reviewed example. These are production choices to test, not measured audience results.

## Focus

Make a short film about **one useful product action and its visible result**. Start with the product's real task. Art, texture and music can make that task more memorable. A catalogue of features needs a different brief.

Most references here are longer than our short-film target. Borrow a useful action or transition, not the whole film's pacing. For a 15–20 second brief, test one complete example before adding another feature.

Use the existing film-lab `seek(t)` renderer, music maps and render checks. This document changes the creative brief and review questions. It does not deploy a Tin service or prove a new generated film passes them.

## 1. Get the missing story facts

Read the product and brand material first. Reuse the user's previous answers. Ask short questions only when these choices are still missing:

- Who is watching, what do they want to do, and what should they understand at the end?
- Which single action can we show, and what real before/after states or assets prove its result?
- Where will it play, how long should it be, and should it use speech or music only?
- Which visual reference or direction fits, and is there a music preference or a look to avoid?

Keep product facts separate from creative preferences. A command, capability, number or UI state needs a source. If the result is an illustration, record that in the brief and avoid presenting it as a recorded product test. Do not add a decorative “example session” or “1 of 5” label to solve an unclear story. If a demonstration needs human help or shows a future capability, keep that condition clear. The Expert Mode label in [441, V13–V14](reviews/441.md) changes how the following chores should be read.

## 2. Write the action before choosing the look

Write a three-line story: the starting situation, the product action, the changed result. The viewer should be able to connect the three using one object, person or task.

| Rule | Why try it | Reference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repeat a situation and change the outcome | The same interrupted night makes the value of incident handling concrete | [004, V01–V05](reviews/004.md) |
| Keep the same asset across formats or edits | A familiar object gives continuity when the interface or material changes | [046, V01–V02](reviews/046.md), [011, V03–V05](reviews/011.md) |
| Finish the opening question with a visible answer | A lead-source question can lead to the specific content row and lead history | [001, V01–V04](reviews/001.md) |
| Show the final action before calling a task complete | A prepared email or calendar form is not yet a sent message or saved event | [476, V02–V05](reviews/476.md) |
| Show useful intermediate outputs | A product sheet and research board make the path from brief to creative output visible | [074, V01–V03](reviews/074.md) |
| Keep the selected item visible through the result | The same named brand kit appears in selection, checkout and confirmation, so the viewer can follow what was bought | [353, V03–V06](reviews/353.md) |
| Match each problem with its changed state | The second half answers the manual stages in the same order; a short film can use just one matched pair | [241, V02 and V04–V06](reviews/241.md) |
| Use a result the film can actually support | A rising percentage can look like proof while hiding the time period and measurement | [100, V03–V04](reviews/100.md) |

An abstract opener is a choice, not a default. If the first two seconds only show a logo or an unexplained shape, compare it with a version that starts on a recognisable problem or result. Do not claim either version “stops the scroll” without a viewer test.

Choose a structure that fits the action. These are alternatives, not three mandatory scenes in every film:

| Structure | Fits a feature that… | What must stay clear | Reference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One object changes | Edits, converts or adapts an asset | The same input and the changed output | [094](reviews/094.md), [046](reviews/046.md) |
| The same situation ends differently | Removes an interruption or a repeated problem | The problem, product action and return to the original activity | [004](reviews/004.md), [116](reviews/116.md) |
| A request becomes a useful artifact | Searches, researches or creates something | The request, relevant work and inspectable result | [054](reviews/054.md), [074](reviews/074.md), [128](reviews/128.md) |

Choose the texture after the structure. A paper, film-grain, illustrated or digital treatment should help the task stay recognisable. Changing the look does not require changing the story, and copying a reference's brand assets is not part of this method.

Art can carry the result with little UI. In [471, V02–V04](reviews/471.md), a request for a yellow-bird story leads to an illustrated book and a bird leaving the page. Keep a concrete detail from the request through the art scene. The scene can explain the idea without proving a live generation or share action.

Texture can mark a change in the task. [295, V01–V04](reviews/295.md) moves from a noisy old screen and paperwork to a clean product view. Its closer samples show the old signal collapsing to a band before the new screen appears. Use that kind of contrast only when the product actually changes the situation being shown.

A physical metaphor can explain a specific action. In [234, V06](reviews/234.md), a miniature invoice shows $2.89 and a shelf label changes from $2.71 to $2.89. That makes price synchronisation visible. For an illustrated film, keep this clear relationship and record in the brief which real product capability it represents.

## 3. Give every scene a job

Use a small scene table before rendering:

| Scene | Viewer learns | Visible action | Same object from previous scene | Proof / source | Sound cue to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [scene] | [one idea] | [specific state change] | [object or deliberate change] | [real asset or supported fact] | [chosen cue, still to be listened to] |

Remove a scene if it adds neither meaning nor an intentional pause. A long style montage can delay the task: [007, V01–V04](reviews/007.md). A technical showcase can explain breadth while leaving the actual workflow unproved: [046, V03–V06](reviews/046.md).

## 4. Use motion to reveal the change

- Enlarge the row, field or object that answers the question. Keep enough surrounding context to show where it belongs. See [001, V03–V04](reviews/001.md). For a physical task, the same idea connects the whole setup to the exact contact point: [103, V01–V03](reviews/103.md).
- Hold the layout steady when comparing categories or values. The changed item should be easy to find. See [003, V03–V05](reviews/003.md).
- Carry a shape or asset through a transition only when it links the two ideas. The enlarging brand shape in [003](reviews/003.md) was inspected in a closer window; do not copy a motion mechanism guessed from two distant stills.
- Use texture changes around a recognisable subject. The subject must remain traceable. See the horse variations in [046, V03–V06](reviews/046.md).
- A mascot can connect action categories, but it cannot replace the result. [174, V03–V05](reviews/174.md) moves from a connection panel to swap/transfer symbols and prompt examples; it never shows the requested output. Keep the visual continuity and complete one task.
- Text can be the object being changed. In [302, V06](reviews/302.md), an “Invert this graphic” instruction changes the displayed word from white on black to black on white. The words demonstrate a feature instead of adding another explanation card.

Use brand fonts and components when they help. Avoid default badges, rounded cards or eyebrow labels that explain nothing. A real product label may be necessary; a decoration that repeats the main text usually is not. Fix unreadable UI by choosing a closer view, not by adding more explanatory text.

## 5. Choose and check sound deliberately

The archive pass does **not** establish which films have good SFX, what instruments they use or how tightly they follow a beat. Do not fill those gaps with a list of imaginary sounds.

For our own film, use the existing music map and a licensed source. Pick a section whose buildup and ending fit the story. Place the important reveal on a measured and listened-to cue. A typing sequence can follow a subdivision, while a result deserves a clear hold. Not every letter or cut needs an added click.

Filter an effect by its job: does it make a selection, movement, impact or state change clearer? Listen in the full mix. Remove effects that merely make every transition louder. Record the source licence and any attribution requirement. An API result or a “free” tag is not proof of permission.

## 6. Review the actual render

1. Review the beginning, middle and ending as one story, with sound off. State what the product does using only what appears.
2. Inspect the whole chronological frame sequence. Open denser windows around fast transitions. A brief shot in [007, 36–39s](reviews/007.md) was missed by one-second samples and found in the closer pass.
3. Check the actual feed-sized version for readable text and a clear focal point. Do not treat a full-screen preview as a phone check.
4. Listen to the final mix and compare meaningful visual events with the sound. If nobody listened, leave this check open.
5. Ask a new viewer what happened and what the product changed. Keep their actual answer. A model's summary is only a rehearsal for this check.
6. Re-render the weak scene and recheck its joins. Passing file decoding, exact timing or loudness checks alone does not prove the film explains the feature.

## What to build next

Try one film that carries the same real product object from problem to action to result. Compare that cut with the current version using the same question: **“What did the product let the person do?”** Keep the clearer structure, then improve its texture and rhythm. The experiment and its result belong in the next film's own notes.
